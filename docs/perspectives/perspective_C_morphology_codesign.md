# Perspective C — The Application School: Robot Morphology Co-Design and Differentiable Simulation for Robot Design

> Literature scout report (verified October 2026). Grades: **A** = verified
> top-tier peer-reviewed paper, canonical work, or well-maintained flagship
> open project; **B** = verified but workshop/docs/secondary venue or applied
> study; **C** = excluded (none included).

Survey context: Physics-informed shape optimization of small mobile robot
bodies. This report covers how robotics works optimize morphology (geometry,
mass distribution, actuator/sensor placement) jointly with control/behavior,
which physics engines and PDE tools carry the "physical world" into the design
loop, and which design variables dominate small mobile robot performance.

## (a) Synthesis: how the physical world enters robot morphology optimization today

- **Simulation gradients are now a first-class design variable.** A family of differentiable simulators — DiffTaichi (ICLR 2020), Brax (2021), Dojo (RSS 2022), MJX/MuJoCo Playground (2025) — lets task performance be differentiated *analytically* through rigid-body contact, soft-body MPM, and even fluid-adjacent physics, so morphology parameters (link lengths, masses, shell geometry) receive exact gradients instead of finite-difference or evolutionary probes. The trade-off is fidelity vs. gradient quality: Dojo exists precisely because reduced-coordinate engines' contact gradients are unreliable, and MJX is documented as best for *small scenes* — which is exactly the regime of a small companion robot.
- **RL-in-the-loop remains the industrial default; black-box search is the outer loop.** RoboGrammar (SIGGRAPH Asia 2020) and modular-robot co-optimization (Sun et al., RSS 2023) wrap a deep-RL or trajectory-optimized controller inside graph/graph-grammar search over topologies, with GNN surrogates pre-screening designs before expensive simulation. Evolutionary body–brain co-evolution (Lipson & Pollack 2000; Cheney et al. 2018; Eiben's morphogenesis program) uses the same pattern with GA outer loops; the 2025 Embodied Co-Design survey (Wang et al.) organizes this whole landscape by brain/morphology/task taxonomies.
- **Optimal control, not RL, when analytic gradients through the *controller* matter.** De Vincenti, Kang & Coros (IROS 2021) differentiate *through* a trajectory optimization to co-optimize leg geometry and gait; the ESA ACT / EPFL mass-limited co-design work (Jeger, Costi et al.) extends differentiable co-design across gravity levels under total-mass budgets — the same budget structure a desktop robot has.
- **PDE-in-the-loop appears only at the discipline boundaries.** Aerodynamic/hydrodynamic shape enters via CFD-in-the-loop (AUV hull optimization cut drag ~4% with tens-to-hundreds of evaluations; adjoint variants exist), and structural/manufacturability physics enters via differentiable FEM-style losses (NeuralTOMO, SIGGRAPH 2025: topology + layer + fiber orientation co-optimized with print constraints as one differentiable loss). Inside mainstream robot co-design, though, the "physics" is still almost exclusively rigid-body contact — no PINN, no PDE residual on the body itself.
- **Real-robot loops are open-source and manual, not gradient-based.** Mini Cheetah (ICRA 2019), ODRI/Solo (RA-L 2020), Open Duck Mini and Reachy Mini (2024–2026) show the small-robot practice: open CAD + 3D printing + MuJoCo/MJX sim-to-real for *control*, but the *body* is iterated by hand — BOM cost, printability, and "does it look/behave right" — with no automated shape-optimization loop. The simulator is used to train the controller for a frozen body, i.e., the co-design loop is broken at the geometry step.

## (c) Design variables that dominate small mobile robot performance

**Footprint geometry (track width, wheelbase) and the support polygon.** Static stability is the distance from the ground projection of the CoM to the nearest edge of the support polygon:

$$d_s = \min_{i}\ \mathrm{dist}\big(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{CoM}\shortparallel},\ \partial S_i\big)$$

Widening track width and wheelbase increases $d_s$ linearly but conflicts with doorway/negotiability and packaging — this is the primary footprint-level trade-off in mobile robot layout design ([Papadopoulos & Rey 1996](http://nereus.mech.ntua.gr/pdf_ps/ICRA96.pdf); rocker-bogie dimensional optimization, [Li et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09544062211027200), optimizes exactly these suspension dimensions for obstacle/slope performance).

**CoM height ("topheaviness").** Under acceleration or on slopes, tipover is governed by the ZMP leaving the support polygon. For distributed point masses:

$$x_{\mathrm{ZMP}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i\left(x_i(\ddot z_i + g) - z_i\ddot x_i\right)}{\sum_i m_i(\ddot z_i + g)}$$

so lowering mass (battery, compute) reduces $z_i \ddot x_i$ terms and stabilizes the ZMP dynamically, not just statically. Papadopoulos & Rey's Force-Angle measure,

$$\alpha = \min_{i}\ \theta_i \cdot \lVert \mathbf{f}_r \rVert,$$

(the minimum angle between the resultant force-moment at the CoM and each tipover-axis normal, scaled by total mass) was introduced precisely because it is *sensitive to CoM height changes* — making it a natural differentiable-ish objective for CoM-placement optimization ([Papadopoulos & Rey 1996](http://nereus.mech.ntua.gr/pdf_ps/ICRA96.pdf); 3D ZMP extensions for mobile manipulators in [ASME JMR 2025](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/computingengineering/article/25/5/051003/1211021/Dynamic-Tip-Over-Avoidance-Method-for-Mobile)). On rough terrain, learned predictors still regress performance from CoM and support-geometry features, confirming CoM height as a dominant variable ([arXiv:2507.12731](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.12731v1)).

**Wheel radius vs. step/obstacle climbing.** A driven rigid wheel of radius $r$ can surmount a step of height $h$ only when $h \lesssim r$ (with the exact limit set by the traction coefficient at the step edge); larger $r$ therefore relaxes mobility constraints but raises the CoM, increases mass and motor torque demands ($\tau \propto r$ for a given drawbar pull), and lowers packaging density. This $r$-vs-$h$-vs-CoM trade-off recurs in every tracked/wheeled/rover design study — e.g., [multi-jointed tracked-wheeled robots](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/13/12/7270) (Gao et al., 2023), DLR's automated rover design balancing suspension dimensions against stability on slopes ([Schäfer et al. 2013](https://elib.dlr.de/86418/1/AUTOMATED_DESIGN_OF_LIGHTWEIGHT_EXPLORATION_ROVERS.pdf)), and the rocker-bogie optimizations ([Li et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09544062211027200)).

**Mass and mass distribution (battery, actuator placement).** Total mass enters every energetic metric; where it sits enters stability and actuator sizing. Energy economy is quantified by cost of transport (specific resistance):

$$\mathrm{CoT} = \varepsilon = \frac{\bar P}{m g \bar v},$$

with wheeled platforms at $\varepsilon \approx 0.1$–$0.5$, and the best engineered legged robots (MIT Cheetah family) at $\varepsilon \approx 0.5$ ([Wensing et al. T-RO 2017](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/119863/Proprioceptive-TRO-RG.pdf); [MIT Biomimetics Lab](https://biomimetics.mit.edu/research/1a9ba04b-d200-4743-b8fc-bf231f3231f0)). Mass-budget-constrained co-design is explicit in [Jeger et al.](https://openreview.net/forum?id=SN9Ucasg05) and in actuator-per-mass methodology of MIT Cheetah/Mini Cheetah ([Katz et al. ICRA 2019](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/ICRA.2019.8793865): 9 kg total, 12 modular actuators chosen to dominate the mass budget).

**Shell surface area and aerodynamics.** Drag scales as $F_D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A v^2$; at companion-robot speeds ($v < 1$ m/s) it is negligible, but for fast mobile robots and AUVs the shell *is* the dominant energy variable, and shape optimization of nose/tail curvature yields measured drag and endurance gains ([Lavimi et al., Ocean Engineering 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0029801824025940): 3.7–4.2% drag reduction; adjoint variants in [Bhuiyan 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11957295)). For desktop robots the same shell area governs convective cooling of compute/battery and dust/intrusion sealing — a thermal-PDE variable the co-design literature ignores.

**Sensor placement (camera FOV, microphones, antennas).** Where sensors sit determines task capability independent of the controller: camera FOV/visibility-cone placement is classically optimized per task ([Triggs 1995](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00548385/document); [Chen 2004](http://personal.cityu.edu.hk/meyfli/Publication/file/CSY_SMC04.pdf); [neural-observation-field hybrid optimization, RAL 2024](https://kevinkaixu.net/papers/cao_ral24_cam.pdf)); microphone-array geometry is optimized with Bayesian methods because the acoustic objective is non-convex ([Zhang et al., MDPI Sensors 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/8/2434)), and robot-head-specific array design shows the head *shape* (baffle geometry) directly modulates localization accuracy ([DLR Rollin' Justin, 2019](https://elib.dlr.de/137626/1/sound_source_loc_for_hum_robots.pdf)); MEMS microphone performance further depends on acoustic cavity/enclosure design (port length, cavity volume, SNR; [SISTC guidance](https://sistc.com/explore-acoustic-cavity-design-mems-microphone-array)). For voice-first companion robots like [Reachy Mini](https://github.com/pollen-robotics/reachy_mini), mic/speaker placement is arguably the most performance-critical geometric variable — and the least formalized.

**Manufacturability and printability.** Print orientation, overhang, minimum wall thickness, part count, and BOM cost bound what morphologies are realizable; NeuralTOMO ([Liu et al., SIGGRAPH 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03779)) shows these can enter as differentiable losses; open hardware projects operationalize them as hard constraints (Open Duck Mini's ["BOM under $400" + print guide](https://github.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Mini); [ODRI's serviceable printed actuator modules](https://open-dynamic-robot-initiative.github.io)).

## (d) Gaps: why PINN-based shape optimization could matter for small companion robots

- **Contact/terrain physics is the weakest link in every simulator used for design.** MJX, Brax, RoboGrammar, and SoftZoo all rely on rigid-body contact approximations (penalty forces, convex relaxations, or smoothed MPM), and Dojo exists because those gradients are untrustworthy. A PINN that learns/resolves the wheel–ground and shell–obstacle contact field (elasticity, friction, partial slip) with PDE residuals could give *geometry-consistent* gradients for the shell and wheel-contact surfaces that reduced-coordinate engines cannot — and it trains on exactly the "small scene" scale where MJX already lives ([MJX docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/mjx.html); [Dojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.00806v5)).
- **The companion robot is a coupled multiphysics body, and current co-design treats it as a rigid skeleton.** Voice-first desktop robots (Reachy Mini, Open Duck Mini) couple enclosure acoustics (mic arrays, speaker ports — [MEMS cavity design](https://sistc.com/explore-acoustic-cavity-design-mems-microphone-array)), structural vibration (motors shaking the mic array), and thermal management of always-on compute inside a palm-sized shell. No existing co-design framework optimizes any of these PDE-governed variables; a PINN formulation with acoustic (Helmholtz/wave) + thermal residual terms could co-optimize shell shape with voice/HRI performance as a single differentiable loss — the same "fold all objectives into one differentiable loss" pattern that made [NeuralTOMO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03779) and [SoftZoo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09555) work.
- **CFD/FEM-in-the-loop is too expensive for the audiences who build small robots.** Even a narrow AUV nose/tail optimization consumes large numbers of RANS solves ([Lavimi et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0029801824025940)), and adjoint pipelines require commercial tooling; hobbyist-scale projects (Open Duck Mini's <$400 BOM, ODRI) iterate bodies by hand precisely because no affordable gradient-bearing physics model of the shell exists. A PINN surrogate amortizing the PDE solve across shape variations would put shell-level physics optimization inside the same $-range workflow as MuJoCo-based policy training.
- **Stability/energy objectives are analytically simple but real bodies are not.** ZMP/force-angle metrics ([Papadopoulos & Rey 1996](http://nereus.mech.ntua.gr/pdf_ps/ICRA96.pdf)) assume rigid bodies and point contacts; the empirical instability of real small robots on rugs, cables, and ramps is what motivates learned stability predictors ([arXiv:2507.12731](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.12731v1)). PINNs are a natural bridge: embed the rigid-body stability metrics as constraints while letting a physics-informed residual model capture the compliant-body effects (footpad deformation, shell flexure, CoM drift under battery sag) that break the analytic metrics.
- **The sim-to-real loop for cheap sensors and actuators is unclosed in current practice.** Open Duck Mini needs actuator identification tooling (BAM) to make its sim match its $30-grade servos, and its roadmap items (eyes, camera, speaker) are planned by hand ([repo README](https://github.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Mini)); Dojo shows gradient-based system identification is the principled route to calibrated models ([Howell et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.00806v5)). A PINN self-model that ingests sparse onboard IMU/mic/current measurements to identify a physics-consistent body model would connect real-robot data to shape optimization — turning the manual "print → test → reprint" loop of open companion robots into a data-driven design loop, which is currently the single biggest methodological gap between the research school (rows 1–11) and the practice school (rows 18–19) of this report.
